# Evidence standard for the independent coaching-engine audit

Prepared by Rowan, Hybrid Coaching Lead, for Ella, Chief of Staff. Research checked on **7 November 2026**. This is a targeted critical review, not a registered systematic review or an exhaustive search. Its purpose is to define defensible audit questions before accepting any LHRF implementation rule. It does not prescribe or activate a replacement programme.

## How to read the confidence statements

**High** means consistent support for the stated general principle from substantial synthesis or a current position stand; it does not mean an exact individual dose is known. **Moderate** means a defensible principle with important limits in population, intervention, measurement or consistency. **Low** means sparse direct evidence, an indirect inference, or coaching convention. Confidence attaches to each claim, not to an entire paper. No formal de novo GRADE assessment was performed.

Trials in general healthy adults, especially novices, cannot establish an optimal programme for an experienced bench competitor. A programme producing some improvement is not necessarily adequate preparation for an advanced athlete's stated goal. Conversely, a dose below a population-average recommendation is not automatically undertraining. Outcomes require adequate exposure, adherence, execution and observed response.

The 2026 ACSM position stand [E1] is the broad contemporary starting point: 137 systematic reviews and more than 30,000 participants **as reported across those reviews**, with searches through October 2024. Those are not necessarily unique participants; reviews overlap. Most contributing studies involved inexperienced lifters. Its recommendations are not a competitive powerlifting manual. The 2026 Pelland dose-response analysis [E3], first online in December 2025, adds newer evidence rather than being covered by the ACSM search.

## 1. Strength specificity and heavy-load exposure

**Standard: high confidence for specificity and a heavier-load advantage in maximal dynamic strength; moderate confidence for applying this to a particular trained competitor; low confidence in any universal exposure count.**

Currier et al.'s 2023 network meta-analysis included 178 strength studies (5,097 participants) and found all evaluated resistance prescriptions superior to no exercise; higher-load prescriptions ranked highest for strength [E2]. Its high-load category was approximately 80% 1RM and above. ACSM 2026 likewise identifies heavier loading (at least 80% 1RM), complete range of motion, multiple sets and sufficient exposure as favorable for voluntary strength [E1]. A network ranking is not proof that the highest-ranked package is uniquely best for every athlete.

For a bench competition, the programme should retain deliberate practice of the competition movement, including the required pause, setup and commands where relevant. This application of specificity is strong coaching reasoning, but the evidence reviewed does not establish a minimum number of paused singles or a mandatory percentage every week. Heavy practice and productive lower-load volume can coexist. A speed or technique session does not substitute automatically for a high-force strength exposure; it can serve a different purpose.

**Audit implications:** count working sets and heavy exposures in the actual generated prescription, using an appropriately evidenced current 1RM or estimated 1RM. Distinguish warm-ups, competition practice, variants, power work and optional work. Inspect prescribed kilograms, reps and effort together. A label such as “heavy” is not evidence of a heavy dose. If the baseline is missing or uncertain, record intensity as uncertain rather than inventing a precise percentage.

**Not established:** a universal weekly minimum of sets at 80%, routine near-maximal testing, a universal strength RPE floor, or complete removal of lower-body strength simply because the athlete also runs.

## 2. Volume, frequency and progressive overload

**Standard: high confidence that repeated adequate loading promotes adaptation and that volume matters; moderate confidence in a dose-response with diminishing returns; low confidence in a single optimal set count or weekly increase.**

Pelland et al. [E3] analyzed 67 studies and 2,058 participants, mostly young men. More weekly volume was associated with greater hypertrophy and strength, with more pronounced diminishing returns for strength. The fractional treatment of indirect sets fit their models best. Increasing frequency showed a strength association with diminishing returns, while the hypertrophy association was compatible with negligible effects. These are aggregate meta-regressions, not randomized tests assigning each individual to a uniquely optimal frequency or volume. “Half a set” is an accounting model, not a biological law.

An earlier frequency review [E4] found no statistically significant frequency advantage in volume-equated comparisons. The newer analysis [E3] provides reason not to reduce frequency solely to a vehicle for volume, but neither analysis proves a universal frequency rule for trained bench athletes. Frequency also distributes practice, time and fatigue. ACSM's at-least-two-session general recommendation and its approximately ten-or-more weekly sets for hypertrophy [E1] should not be converted into a mandatory ten-set bench-strength floor.

Progressive overload means that the meaningful stimulus can increase as capacity increases: kilograms, repetitions, appropriately dosed sets, range, technical execution or density when suitable. It does not require increasing every variable or prescribing a fixed percentage increase each week. Constant relative effort with rising absolute load can be progression; a temporary hold can be appropriate. Increases should be evaluated against the athlete's actual ability to complete the intended task.

**Audit implications:** distinguish primary-lift direct sets from support/indirect work; inspect totals before and after recovery, scheduling and time fitting. Show both planned load/repetition progression and observed performance. A session disappearing during fitting can remove the only remaining squat/hinge practice even if the calendar is technically feasible. Zero planned load growth alone does not prove failure if the programme deliberately uses athlete-calibrated effort; establish what execution guidance actually does.

**Not established:** every athlete needs 10–20 sets per muscle, more is always better, a fixed 2.5% increase works indefinitely, or every week must exceed the previous week's tonnage.

## 3. Repetitions, intensity, RPE and proximity to failure

**Standard: high confidence that failure is not required for strength; moderate confidence that proximity to failure is more influential for hypertrophy than maximal strength; low confidence in exact universal RPE cutoffs.**

Loads, reps and effort are related but not interchangeable. A low-repetition set can be easy; a high-repetition set can be very demanding. RPE based on repetitions in reserve is an estimate whose meaning depends on athlete understanding, exercise and task. An RPE 8 target is commonly used to mean approximately two repetitions remaining, but this is an execution convention, not a precise measurement of physiological stress.

Robinson et al. [E5] found that estimated closer proximity to failure was associated with more hypertrophy, whereas strength gains were similar across a broad estimated-RIR range. The analysis was explicitly exploratory, with modest model fit; RIR was reconstructed from study descriptions, not consistently measured. ACSM [E1] similarly concludes that momentary failure is not necessary and explicitly states that evidence is insufficient to quantify exact RIR/RPE targets. Its practical suggestion of near-failure effort or roughly 2–3 RIR should not be presented as a proven universal optimum.

Low-effort work can be useful for familiarization, technique, power, recovery or tapering. Persistent very low effort across almost all working work may fail a trained athlete's development goal, but the reviewed evidence does not identify a single “below this RPE is useless” threshold. A heavy single can be specific without being a maximal grind, while a low-RPE accessory set may contribute less hypertrophy than an adequately challenging one.

**Audit implications:** evaluate RPE alongside actual load, repetitions, quality and the session's purpose. Check whether estimated baselines are anchored to comparable executions. Do not treat an unrelated machine, warm-up, optional set, failed rep or unverified legacy record as interchangeable evidence. Exclude those records for evidence-quality reasons, not because an arbitrary RPE band alone defines valid training.

**Not established:** all sets must reach failure; RPE 6 cannot build strength; all athletes should train at RPE 8; one hard session proves the block should be rewritten; a small RPE overshoot demonstrates maladaptation.

## 4. Power and velocity

**Standard: high confidence in training fast force production when power is the target; moderate confidence in selected velocity-based approaches; low confidence that velocity hardware or a fixed speed-day template is necessary.**

Power is force multiplied by velocity. Heavy strength work and rapid movement tasks develop overlapping but different qualities. ACSM [E1] favors fast concentric execution, moderate loads and low-to-moderate repetition volume for power outcomes, while noting exercise-specific limits. Its 30–70% 1RM summary is a broad range, not a universal bench-speed loading law. Heavy external loads move slowly even with maximal intended acceleration, so observed bar speed and intent must be distinguished.

A 2026 meta-analysis comparing velocity-based and percentage-based resistance training included 17 studies and 348 trained participants [E6]. It found small benefits for jump performance and change of direction, but no statistically clear maximal-strength advantage (SMD 0.21, 95% CI −0.01 to 0.43) or sprint advantage. Jump performance is not identical to measured muscle power, and none of these results proves that a hybrid bench competitor must use a velocity transducer.

**Audit implications:** a speed session should specify intent, manageable fatigue and how repetition quality is judged, with a practical fallback if velocity is unavailable. A lighter load plus a “speed” name is insufficient to establish a power stimulus. Adding a mandatory speed day can displace the athlete's only useful heavy or volume exposure; establish its purpose and opportunity cost first.

## 5. Periodisation, fatigue management and tapering

**Standard: moderate confidence for purposeful variation and reduced fatigue before competition; low confidence in fixed deload schedules or uniquely optimal strength-taper formulas.**

Moesgaard et al. [E7] found a small strength advantage for periodized over non-periodized volume-equated training (ES 0.31, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.57), and for undulating over linear approaches (0.31, 0.02 to 0.61), with no clear hypertrophy advantage. The trained subgroup favoring undulation was imprecise. ACSM's broader synthesis [E1] did not find a consistent independent periodisation advantage and emphasizes progressive overload. This is a reason to preserve purposeful structure and judge results, not proof that complex cycles are necessary or that all periodisation is ineffective.

Bell et al.'s deload guidance [E8] is a Delphi consensus of experienced coaches, with 34 participants initially and 21 in the final round. It provides a useful definition and design considerations, not a causal demonstration that a deload every fourth week improves outcomes. Coleman et al.'s randomized study [E9] compared continuous training with one week of **complete resistance-training cessation** midway through a nine-week high-volume programme in 39 trained adults. Continuous training produced better lower-body strength, with no appreciable hypertrophy, power or endurance advantage for cessation. That does not show that all reduced-volume deloads are harmful; it does undermine an automatic “rest improves all subsequent gains” claim.

For endurance competition, Wang et al.'s 14-study synthesis [E10] supports reduced volume while maintaining some intensity, with an effective range around a 41–60% reduction over no more than 21 days. The review includes sports and taper designs beyond a recreational 10K; subgroup averages do not identify a precise individual optimum. “Maintain intensity” means retaining an appropriate stimulus, not retaining the full quantity of hard work.

Strength-specific taper evidence is thinner [E11]. Winwood et al.'s 2026 systematic review [E12] describes seven studies of **self-reported practices**, mostly surveys. Common reports included roughly 40–45% volume reductions, short tapers and a last bench session several days before competition. These are descriptions of practice, not experimental proof that a particular last-heavy-day interval is optimal. The same “taper week” rule should not automatically govern bench, deadlift and 10K preparation.

**Audit implications:** inspect the actual competition date and the full work performed in the preceding days; retain sport-specific familiarity while reducing avoidable fatigue. Check for two independent taper/recovery rules compounding and deleting useful work. Evaluate remaining preparation from the athlete's current state: a short late-stage block cannot replace months of missing strength exposure or running base. Do not diagnose overtraining syndrome from a generated high workload; report excessive-load or recovery-risk concerns.

## 6. Running intensity distribution and easy-volume development

**Standard: moderate-to-high confidence that substantial tolerable low-intensity training is useful within successful endurance development; moderate confidence that several intensity distributions can work; low confidence in universal 80/20 or mileage rules.**

Casado et al.'s review [E13] describes predominantly pyramidal intensity distributions in highly trained/elite runners, with event- and phase-specific variation. It is largely evidence about what successful athletes do, not proof that their absolute mileage or exact distribution should be copied to a novice or strength-priority hybrid.

Oliveira et al.'s 2024 meta-analysis [E14] found a small polarized-training advantage for VO2peak (SMD 0.24, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.48), particularly in shorter interventions and highly trained athletes, but no clear superiority for time-trial performance (−0.01, −0.28 to 0.25), time to exhaustion or threshold velocity/power. Rosenblat et al.'s 2025 individual-participant-data network meta-analysis [E15] included 13 studies and 348 participants, 296 male and 52 female. It found no statistically significant polarized-versus-pyramidal difference in VO2max or time trial performance. Performance-level subgroup findings are hypotheses for tailoring, not an automatic model-selection algorithm.

The denominator matters. Time in physiological zones, kilometres at a pace, and sessions classified by their hardest portion can produce very different “80/20” values for the same week. Warm-ups, recovery jogs and cooldowns must be counted. Easy running should be easy relative to the athlete's current physiology, conditions and recovery; a percentage of an aspirational race pace is not sufficient evidence.

Volume should start from an actual recent tolerated base, not a generic target event table. A programme may undertrain an experienced runner by cutting a large stable base to a small default. It may overload a new runner even with a “reasonable” weekly percentage if an individual long run or intensity change is disproportionately large. Frandsen et al.'s 2025 prospective cohort [E16] found associations between session-distance spikes and overuse injury, but not week-to-week ratios. A session more than 10% longer than the longest run in the preceding 30 days was associated with increased injury rate. This is observational evidence; it does not establish a universal safe threshold or a causal 10% progression rule, and its session comparison is not a 10% weekly-mileage rule.

**Audit implications:** report weekly running kilometres and minutes, easy and quality work, longest session, and abrupt changes from measured history. Where an output lacks enough detail to calculate actual time in zones, state that the intensity distribution is unidentifiable rather than asserting compliance from labels. Prevent easy-volume additions from becoming automatic pace increases.

## 7. Threshold, tempo and interval prescription

**Standard: high confidence that intensity is multidimensional and relative to current capacity; moderate confidence for combining specific quality with recoverable volume; low confidence in exact universal pace offsets or interval recipes.**

Jamnick et al. [E17] show why percentages of maximal anchors can place different athletes in different physiological intensity domains. LT1/VT/GET and the heavy-to-severe boundary are not interchangeable concepts; critical speed/power has a different evidential basis from fixed percentages of maximum heart rate or an arbitrary “threshold” pace. Their review concerns validity of intensity assignment and explicitly cautions that acute responses do not prove superior long-term adaptation. It does not require every user to undergo laboratory testing.

“Tempo” is an ambiguous coaching label. A defensible prescription identifies intended stimulus and execution: duration or distance, work-bout intensity, repetition count, recovery duration/intensity, warm-up/cooldown and how to adjust if effort is inconsistent. A 10K pace is not automatically a sustainable threshold pace: the duration of a 10K varies greatly between athletes. Short intervals can use a faster pace because their recovery and accumulated work differ; pace alone cannot establish their training cost.

**Audit implications:** trace where pace comes from, whether it reflects demonstrated current fitness, and whether workout duration matches that assumption. Use recent comparable evidence and practical effort/talk guidance where valid measurement is unavailable. Inspect work minutes as well as whole-session distance. Rest intervals and surrounding easy running affect both feasibility and stimulus. There is no evidence here that all hybrids need two quality runs every week.

## 8. Race-specific pacing and progression

**Standard: high confidence for keeping current ability distinct from goals; moderate confidence for a progressive, race-relevant approach; low confidence in universal event-conversion formulas or fixed countdown rules.**

A target time describes the desired outcome. It is not evidence of current capacity. A recent maximal race, a well-executed comparable field test, or multiple coherent training performances can inform pace. Each requires date, distance, course/conditions and context. Heart-rate or device data should be used only if actually available and interpretable. A historical PB may be stale; a marathon result can be affected by endurance durability, conditions and pacing, so a converted 10K prediction is an estimate, not a measurement. Jamnick [E17] supports caution about intensity assignment; it does **not** validate or invalidate a particular Riegel exponent (such as 1.06), an inferred one-hour threshold pace, or the size of marathon-to-10K prediction error. Model-specific validation remains unresolved in this review.

Race preparation can gradually introduce an appropriate amount of target-specific work while retaining aerobic development and recovery. The reviewed evidence does not establish that a 10K programme must progress through a fixed sequence of named workouts, that goal pace is safe merely because race day is close, or that every athlete must achieve a fixed long-run distance. Feasibility constraints can make an ambitious target unrealistic; deleting the training and retaining the original performance promise is not a defensible solution.

**Audit implications:** show current-performance pace, prescribed pace and goal pace separately. A discrepancy can be a legitimate stimulus, stale evidence or a generation defect; inspect the workout and evidence before classifying it. Reassess from comparable execution over time, including conditions and perceived exertion, without inventing a new PB from every easy run.

## 9. Concurrent strength/endurance interference

**Standard: moderate-to-high confidence that concurrent training can develop both capacities; moderate confidence that explosive strength and session arrangement deserve particular care; low confidence in universal separation windows.**

Schumann et al. [E18] synthesized 43 studies comparing concurrent training with an identical strength prescription without aerobic work. Average effects did not show statistically significant deficits for maximal strength (SMD −0.06, 95% CI −0.20 to 0.09) or hypertrophy (−0.01, −0.16 to 0.18). Explosive strength was attenuated (−0.28, −0.48 to −0.08), especially with same-session training. The intervals for maximal strength remain compatible with a small negative effect; nonsignificance does not prove no interference in every athlete. Highly trained competitors and very high combined volumes are not fully represented.

The separated-session subgroup used at least three hours, but that does not establish three hours as a magic safe minimum. Acute residual fatigue, exercise order, intensity, nutrition, sleep and the athlete's priority remain relevant. Prioritizing the goal discipline early in a combined visit is reasonable; a universal rule is not justified by this review alone.

Strength can support running. Llanos-Lagos et al.'s review [E19] found improvements in running economy with high-load, plyometric or combined strength methods, with moderate certainty for high-load and lower certainty for combined methods. Economy is not itself a guaranteed race-time improvement, and the evidence does not justify abruptly adding heavy or plyometric loads to an inexperienced lifter. Conversely, fear of interference does not justify eliminating all useful lower-body training.

**Audit implications:** inspect temporal proximity of hard runs and demanding leg work, consecutive hard days, total lower-body loading and which sessions get removed. A bench-priority athlete should not be governed by exactly the same conflict policy as a running-priority athlete. Preserve a meaningful minimum stimulus or expose the tradeoff explicitly when constraints make all goals impossible.

## 10. Post-marathon recovery and individual variability

**Standard: high confidence that a marathon can create meaningful acute fatigue and tissue stress; moderate confidence that recovery varies substantially; low confidence in a universal restriction duration or strength-dose cap.**

Martínez-Navarro et al.'s randomized recovery trial [E20] included 64 marathon finishers (54 men, 10 women; mean finish approximately 3:35). Forty-minute running or elliptical sessions at a prescribed moderate intensity began 48 hours after the race and were repeated at 96 and 144 hours. Blood muscle-damage markers did not differ significantly between intervention groups; the running group showed better squat-jump recovery than resting at 96 hours. This is evidence against assuming all controlled activity harms every recovering runner. A nonsignificant marker comparison is not a safety or noninferiority demonstration. It does **not** establish that heavy squats, near-maximal bench, intervals or another race are safe at 48 hours, or that a finish-time-selected group represents slow or injured recoverers.

Braschler et al.'s 2025 narrative review [E21] describes multiple organ-system responses and variable recovery; many biomarker changes are short lived, but some injuries and functional deficits persist. This is contextual evidence, not a systematic dose-response analysis of return to heavy lifting. Normal biomarkers, absence of soreness and restored performance are related but distinct observations. Neither a calendar cutoff nor feeling subjectively recovered proves all tissues are ready for maximal work.

**Fast scenario:** normal walking/stairs, resolving soreness, good sleep/appetite and tolerable easy sessions justify reassessing staged return rather than preserving an indefinite restrictive flag. **Slow scenario:** pain, poor function, continued performance suppression or poor recovery justify holding or reducing affected work even after the default window expires. These are clinical/coaching decision examples, not validated numerical thresholds. Significant injury symptoms or systemic illness warrant immediate intervention and appropriate clinical assessment.

**Audit implications:** restrictions need a source, affected activities, start/event date, review evidence and a path to reassessment. Differentiate lower-body impact from unaffected work while respecting systemic fatigue. A fixed profile observation should not silently bind every future week; nor should its arbitrary expiry force immediate full loading. Synthetic fast/slow cases test behavior, not actual human recovery outcomes.

## 11. Training adaptation and athlete feedback

**Standard: moderate-to-high confidence that subjective reports are useful; moderate confidence in combining them with performance/adherence/context; low confidence in a single optimal review interval or automated threshold.**

Saw et al.'s systematic review [E22] found subjective well-being measures sensitive to acute and chronic training changes; subjective and objective measures often did not correlate. It does not establish that one low mood, high RPE or sore session should trigger a programme rewrite. It also does not justify requiring an objective performance decline before believing reported pain or persistent poor well-being. Objective evidence should inform interpretation, not veto the athlete's experience.

The proposed stability framework is a **governance and coaching design choice**, not a trial-proven biological threshold: a session report first informs safe execution and monitoring; a weekly reflection seeks patterns; roughly 2–4 weeks of repeated, comparable evidence can support a normal programme review. A literal 14-day lockout can be harmful when an injury emerges or an obviously mismatched prescription repeatedly fails. Waiting four weeks is also inappropriate for an imminent event if the current plan is clearly incoherent. Conversely, a single unusually good session should not automatically increase the whole block.

Separate execution adjustments within an agreed range from changing the programme's structure, goals, dose policy or progression rules. Normal structural changes require an explicit rationale, preview of consequences and athlete approval. Emergency safety actions may be immediate; they still need provenance and follow-up. Missing feedback is uncertainty, not a negative score, consent or confirmation of recovery.

**Audit implications:** reproduce actual proposal creation, carry-forward and approval behavior. Identify whether a rule changes today's load, merely displays advice, proposes a future change, or modifies an approved plan. Count repeated evidence by comparable exposure and date, not raw rows that can include duplicates, warm-ups or edits to old records. Preserve programme identity and history. Do not equate warning spam with actual programme churn.

## Proposed audit decision standard

| Finding class | What qualifies | What does not qualify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmed implementation defect | Reproducible behavior contradicts its input contract or a necessary constraint, using the audited code and fixture; scope and consequence shown. | Disagreement with a preferred coaching template; a warning label alone. |
| Evidence-supported improvement | Actual output misses a defensible goal-specific principle and a smaller change has a coherent benefit/tradeoff. | Converting a population average, association or expert consensus into a universal cutoff. |
| Unresolved coaching decision | Multiple defensible doses/distributions exist, or athlete evidence/provenance is inadequate. | Silently choosing a policy and describing it as scientifically compelled. |

The acceptable engine is not required to mirror one coach's favored split. It should produce a coherent, progressive, goal-specific and practically executable dose; explain compromises; use honest evidence and uncertainty; preserve meaningful training under constraints; and adapt with stability and athlete agency.

## Research verification notes

Searches combined source names and subject terms for the eleven requested domains, then directly opened PubMed, PubMed Central, publisher, institutional or Europe PMC records. Query families included resistance-training prescription/dose response; proximity to failure; velocity-based training; periodized versus non-periodized volume-equated training; deload trials/consensus; endurance taper meta-analysis; polarized/pyramidal/threshold intensity distribution; running intensity-domain validity; concurrent interference; running economy; marathon recovery; and subjective athlete monitoring. Publications dated after the audit date were not used.

No source was accepted solely from a commercial coaching summary. Some search results displayed dynamic dates from 2026 for older studies; citation years above come from the indexed/publisher publication, not result freshness. Citations are not invented and no full-text access is claimed where only abstracts were accessible. An external broad research run was used as a supplemental search aid; conclusions above rest on the individually checked records listed here. The broad run's stronger recommendations for fixed return-to-training windows, deload intervals and modality-separation durations were not adopted: they exceeded what the individually assessed evidence established.
